Edited with Maya Avis and Maria Sapignoli.

Debates about big data surveillance and artificial intelligence in governance open up questions about the role of technology in producing the knowledge that states use to govern. The contributions to this volume examine the socio-technical assemblages behind surveillance by criminal justice institutions, especially the digital tools at the heart of modern state bureaucracies.

Drawing on ethnographic research in settings around the world, the chapters engage with technology's promises of transformation, look at the ways of thinking that become embedded through technologies, consider the political economy driving the expansion of security technologies, and examine how people at the margins experience surveillance.
