A welcome search for evidence

Last Thursday, on 12 November 2026, HM Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services (HMICFRS) published its inspection of how police use retrospective facial recognition available through the Police National Database (PND). Alongside it, the Home Office surveyed users of the system on their experience. Together with the testing for accuracy and bias carried out by the National Physical Laboratory (NPL) in 2025, these provide desperately needed transparency and evidence on a technology that has rightly received close scrutiny by civil liberties groups (e.g., Liberty, Privacy International, Big Brother Watch).

In use since 2013, the reports show how deeply facial recognition is now built into everyday policing. Forces ran 291,341 facial searches in the year to March 2026, about 24,000 a month (this is solely on the PND; some forces operate their own separate systems). An officer uploads a still image, often from CCTV and increasingly from doorbell cameras, and the system returns a list of possible matches from more than 20 million custody photographs.

In NPL's tests, the algorithm put the right person first in 3,510 of 3,513 searches: 99.9%. But that figure only counts searches where the person was in the database. It does not tell us how often a search for someone who is not on file produces a convincing-looking wrong face, how often a reviewer picks that face, or what investigators do next. At this scale, accuracy becomes a numbers game. Search often enough, for enough people who are not on file or are caught on grainy footage, and even a very good system produces a steady stream of wrong faces. What's more, these faces will look strikingly similar to the one searched for because finding similar faces is what the technology is optimised for.

Unfortunately, one of HMICFRS's recent findings was that most police forces do not record or audit their use of facial recognition, which makes it impossible to assess both the successes, beyond anecdotal evidence and a broad majority of respondents in the Home Office survey judging RFR useful, and the failures. Yet a third of surveyed users were wrong or unsure about whether a match meets the legal standard for identification (it does not).

By way of a back-of-the-envelope calculation this blog post assesses the latter issue, how often might a false positive be used as an investigative lead resulting in police contact for an unrelated individual?

A cautious estimate of how big the problem could be

We can step-by-step estimate how many searches could turn out false positive results resulting in false investigative leads. For each step, I provide an estimate and, where values are not known, take the cautious end of what the evidence allows, so that the final figure is deliberately restrictive.

We start with all 291,341 searches. This is HMICFRS's count of PND facial searches in the year to March 2026. While the report says the bulk of these searches was for volume crimes like shop theft, there is no further breakdown for these searches. It may be that some of these would have been in search of victims or for identifying someone in a mental health crisis; the identity of missing persons is usually known. From a Police Scotland audit we know that around 96% of searches are for suspects (that is excluding searches for "vulnerable adult", "child abuse", and vetting searches) and 42% of searches are repeat searches of an image already searched. Let us assume that this is also true for England and Wales.

To be conservative, we therefore exclude these in addition to the repeat searches estimated with the Scottish data. HMICFRS reports that 74% of searches return at least one face, usually 9 to 15. That's close to searches putting roughly faces in front of reviewers each year (appearances on lists, not necessarily different people), nearly all of them people who had nothing to do with the incident but happen to have a custody photo on the PND.

Now, how many of these searches do not include the right person? Probably around . Two things decide whether the right person is on a list: whether they have a custody photo, and whether the algorithm finds them. The NPL measured how often the algorithm returns a wrong face at different settings. In its tests, a search for someone not in the database returned a wrong face 32.6% of the time at a threshold of 0.7, 13.1% at 0.75, 2.7% at 0.8, 0.2% at 0.85 and never at 0.9. But it used good-quality photos and a much smaller database than PND (about 180,000 images, against more than 20 million). A bigger database means more chances of a look-alike, while poorer images tend to produce weaker matches with everyone. So the test rates do not apply directly to real searches.

Instead, the tests show how sharply wrong matches fall as the threshold rises. The setting the police originally used corresponds to about 0.7 (32.6% wrong faces in NPL's tests). The stricter setting tried briefly in late 2024 corresponds to about 0.82 (roughly 1.2% wrong faces, according to the NPCC). When the stricter setting was tried, the share of searches returning anyone fell by three-quarters (from 56% to 14%, according to the NPCC's 2024 figures). Because look-alikes almost disappear at that setting while good-quality matches survive, most of what remained must have been genuine matches. But some genuine matches were lost too, especially from blurry footage. How many is not published, but forces reported a significant loss of useful results. How many wrongful arrests and interviews under caution?

If you think these were already a lot of assumptions, strap in because the unknowns become only bigger from here. But hopefully you agree that, so far, we have been conservative and searches returning lists of false matches is probably on the lower end of possibilities.

Now, how often would these searches have led to arrest or interview under caution? This heavily depends on how much officers scrutinise the results. From laboratory research, we know that non-experts (students and passport examiners) pick the wrong match more than 50% of the time, while specialist facial examiners are still wrong 30% of the time. Of course, investigators have more information at hand than just the match. A match for a shop theft in Reading with a registered home address in Newcastle would probably prompt closer checks. So, perhaps we filter this list further down with a guess: We know that in the case of Alvi Choudhury a false match led to him being arrested for a burglary 100 miles away (about 80 miles as the crow flies). That is quite a long distance away from the crime. Under these very conservativechosen conditions, there are still about searches annually for which investigators will have to make further inquiries. Matches should only be treated as intelligence and further evidence is required. However, from the Home Office survey we know that about one in ten users (10.1% for frequent users and 8.7% for infrequent users with less than 60 searches a year; 73 of 779 respondents) wrongly said a match meets the legal standard for identification, and about one in three were wrong or unsure (263 of 779). HMICFRS even found in one force's focus group that more than half did not know that results were for intelligence only. And the survey targeted trained PND licence holders, who run the searches. The investigators who submit images and decide what happens next were not asked, and HMICFRS found their training insufficient, so among them the share may well be higher. As a cautious rule, we assume that only of wrong leads are acted on as if they were evidence, which means about cases a year in which someone is visited, interviewed under caution or arrested on a wrong match. This is an illustrative scenario, not a minimum. It assumes that the other wrong leads never lead to contact, but also that every wrong lead reaching someone who treats it as evidence does. These cases do not affect just anyone. They affect those who have a photograph on the PND because they have previously been suspected of a crime. Among them the burden is uneven: in NPL's tests, Black women were wrongly returned as candidates far more often than White people. Being returned more often is not yet the same as a higher risk of wrongful arrest, but unfortunately forces do not record whether it becomes one.

The post's scenario rests on a series of deliberately cautious assumptions:

  • More searches exist as some forces use their own systems
  • Repeat searches removed, including about 13,000 resubmissions by BTP, although repeats can also produce wrong matches
  • Excluded searches for victims, vulnerable people or vetting
  • Wrong picks from lists that also contain the right person are left out
  • Reviewers accept a wrong face only 30% of the time, the low end of laboratory research with clear sample photos.
  • A wrong face is kept only if the top match lives within 25 miles of the crime; nobody looks further down the list and nobody accepts a distant face, although the Choudhury case shows this happens with Hampshire officers arresting him on behalf of Thames Valley police.
  • Only the one in ten users who think a match is evidence act on a wrong lead; for everyone else, further enquiries always catch the error.

What could be done about this?

Loosen the assumptions above and the number of people contacted on a wrong match quickly reaches thousands a year. The real figure is unknown because nobody records it. HMICFRS recommends that police forces should make more of the search facility. But even more searches will mean even more false positives. So, what could be done about this?

  • Most importantly, police forces have to start tracking the outcomes from searches. HMICFRS calls on police to "routinely collect, analyse and review retrospective facial recognition information" to evaluate its use, currently we know too little about how effective it is beyond officers' opinions and, as argued here, how high the risk of false identifications is. For every search, that means recording whether it was new or a repeat, the threshold and the candidates' rankings, who selected a candidate and whether a second reviewer checked it independently, what corroborating evidence was obtained, whether the person was contacted, interviewed, arrested or eliminated, and the eventual outcome.
  • Police forces should evaluate if blind second review of matches or centralised teams kept separate from the investigation might develop a stronger capacity of handling retrospective facial recognition (see also recommendation 14 from HMICFRS).
  • The threshold could be adjusted again to reduce wrong leads. However, it also means less true matches.
  • The legislator could limit the types of crime facial recognition is applied to. For example, excluding shoplifting would likely severely reduce the number of searches and therefore false positives.
  • An improved algorithm may reduce the number of false positives. NPL tested a system trialled by Essex Police and Cheshire Constabulary which found all true matches and returned no wrong images, offering usually only one (or zero) match per search. However, this is based on high-quality photos not CCTV and doorbell camera footage.

The numbers show that even with an algorithm with reportedly 99.9% accuracy, just the sheer volume of searches means that there are bound to be errors. It is crucial for trust in policing that there is more transparency around this and that legislators take this into account in drafting the long-awaited legislation on facial recognition.

Recording investigative dead ends may seem superfluous for police purposes. A lead that goes nowhere is simply dropped, and this is probably the non-conspiratorial reason why these numbers don't exist. But logging them would do more than serve transparency. Investigators who have to note that a facial recognition lead pointed to the wrong person may stop to reflect on how much weight they gave it, and the records would help identify officers who are perhaps too reliant on the algorithm.

We know already of two cases of false identifications leading to wrongful arrests: Alvi Choudhury, arrested by Thames Valley Police in January 2026 for a burglary in a city he had never visited, and a man held for 13 hours by South Wales Police, who later paid him damages. According to his solicitor, Mr Choudhury's photo was only in the database because of an earlier wrongful arrest. HMICFRS describes another case, in which a man was circulated for arrest over a series of shoplifting offenses and turned out not to match the image. It is likely that across the country there are many more cases of wrongful arrests of people that to the police are the 'usual suspects' and that themselves may be so used to police contact that the injustice they experienced was not out of the ordinary - perhaps this is why the capacity has existed for 13 years without any larger scandals. While half the public worry about false identification of suspects (49% in the Home Office's own survey of public attitudes), the population that is most affected is probably all too often deemed deserving of the unwarranted attention.

HMICFRS has made 16 recommendations, most due by the end of 2027: national standards for collecting and reviewing data on how searches are used, mandatory training for investigators, centralised search teams, a statutory code of practice and independent oversight. This is a genuinely good start. I hope the next round of work will be able to not only ask whether officers want the technology but also how well it is used, and who pays when it is not. Not knowing how often this goes wrong is a reason to demand better evidence, not a reason to assume the risk is small.
